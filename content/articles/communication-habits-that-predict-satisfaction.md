The intuitive model of a good relationship is a compatibility model. You find
someone who wants the same things, and the relationship works because you match.

Longitudinal research on couples points somewhere less romantic. Measures of
similarity — personality, interests, background — predict relationship outcomes
weakly. Measures of *how partners interact during disagreement* predict them
substantially better. The same fights happen in relationships that last and
relationships that do not. What differs is how they are conducted.

Below are five habits that show up repeatedly in that literature. They are
observable, which is what makes them useful — you can notice yourself doing them,
or not doing them.

## 1. Repair attempts, and whether they are accepted

A repair attempt is any move that de-escalates during friction: a joke, a
softened tone, an admission of being partly wrong, a change of subject, a hand on
the arm.

The important finding is that the *content* of the repair matters less than
whether it lands. Couples who stay together make and accept repair attempts at
higher rates; couples who struggle make them and miss them. Missing them tends to
compound — a repair attempt that fails usually escalates matters, because it
reads as rejection on top of the original disagreement.

**The observable version:** when tension rises, does anyone try to lower it, and
does the other person let them?

## 2. Criticism of behaviour versus criticism of character

Complaints about what someone did are not the same as claims about who they are.
"There were dishes in the sink again" is a behavioural statement that can be
acted on. "You never think about anyone but yourself" is a character claim, and
it invites defence rather than change.

Research on conflict distinguishes complaint from criticism precisely here. The
first is normal and often necessary. The second predicts deterioration, because
it reframes a specific incident as evidence about a person's worth.

**The observable version:** count the sentences that start with "you always" or
"you never".

## 3. Turning toward bids

A bid is a small request for attention — a comment about something seen out of
the window, a sigh, a question about the day. Turning toward means responding:
looking up, engaging, acknowledging.

The finding that surprises people is scale. In observational work, couples who
remained together turned toward their partner's bids at a much higher rate than
couples who separated — and the bids themselves were almost entirely trivial.
The pattern is built out of unremarkable moments, not grand gestures.

**The observable version:** when your partner says something that does not
strictly require an answer, what do you do?

## 4. Regulated versus flooded

Physiological flooding — heart rate up, adrenaline, the feeling of being unable
to think clearly — makes productive conversation physically difficult. Under
flooding, people become less accurate at reading the other person and more likely
to say things they do not mean.

Couples who handle conflict well more often take a break before that point, and
come back. It is not avoidance; it is sequencing. A twenty-minute break with a
genuine return is different from walking out, and the difference is whether you
come back.

**The observable version:** do you notice the moment you stop thinking clearly,
and does anyone call a pause?

## 5. Ownership of the other person's perspective

This is the habit that correlates most strongly with satisfaction in the research
on what makes partners feel understood: the ability to state the other person's
position accurately, in a way they would recognise as fair, without conceding
your own.

It is not agreement. It is demonstrated comprehension. People who feel accurately
understood fight less about the same things, because the disagreement is narrower
than it appeared — often it turns out the fight was about being heard rather than
about the original subject.

**The observable version:** try stating your partner's view before your own. If
they say "that's not what I meant", you have something to work with.

## What this list is not

These are patterns from research on couples, not a guarantee, and not a diagnosis
of your relationship. A partner who is consistently contemptuous, controlling, or
who makes you feel unsafe is not a communication-habits problem, and no technique
on this list addresses it. That situation calls for support from outside the
relationship, and possibly from a professional.

For everyone else — which is most people, most of the time — the useful takeaway
is that the list is boring. Repair attempts, complaint instead of criticism,
turning toward bids, taking a break, restating the other perspective. None of it
is dramatic, and all of it is observable, which means it is practiceable.
