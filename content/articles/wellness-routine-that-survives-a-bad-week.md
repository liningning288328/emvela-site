Almost everyone can hold a routine together for two weeks. The failure point is
not motivation, it is design: routines are usually built for a good week, with a
free evening, decent sleep and no crisis. When a bad week arrives, they have
exactly two settings — full and abandoned — and abandoned is the only one that
fits.

The fix is structural. Build a routine with tiers, define in advance what
dropping to the next tier looks like, and treat the drop as the system working
rather than the system failing.

## Start with the floor, not the ceiling

Write down the version you could do on your worst realistic day. Not your
theoretical worst — a bad week, not a catastrophe. Then:

- **Tier 1 — the floor.** Ten minutes total. This is what you do when everything
  is going wrong.
- **Tier 2 — the normal week.** A version you can hold alongside ordinary work
  and family demands. Be honest, not aspirational.
- **Tier 3 — the good week.** What you do when you have time and energy.

The common mistake is making Tier 2 aspirational. If Tier 2 is not achievable in
a normal week, it is Tier 3, and you are running a two-tier system where the
floor does not exist.

## What belongs in the floor

Tier 1 should consist only of things that are load-bearing for everything else.
For most people that is a short list:

1. **A fixed wake time.** Not a fixed bedtime — a fixed wake time. It anchors
   the circadian rhythm and it is the one thing that keeps the rest of the
   structure from drifting.
2. **Water and something to eat before coffee.** Small, unglamorous, and it
   prevents the mid-morning crash that people then try to solve with more coffee.
3. **Ten minutes of movement.** Walking counts. The point is the signal, not the
   training effect.
4. **Two minutes of daylight, outdoors if possible.** Ideally early. Morning
   light is the strongest available anchor for sleep timing.

That is the whole floor. It fits in fifteen minutes and it holds the structure
together when nothing else happens.

## Make the transition explicit

The tier system only works if dropping a level is a decision rather than a
slippage. Two mechanisms help:

- **A weekly check-in.** Ten minutes, same day each week. Which tier did this
  week actually run at? If it was Tier 1, was that because the week was hard or
  because Tier 2 is wrong? Those are different problems with different fixes.
- **A named trigger for dropping down.** For example: "two nights under six
  hours' sleep, or a deadline week" means Tier 1 automatically. Deciding in
  advance removes the negotiation, and the negotiation is where routines die.

The reverse also needs a trigger — a time to step back up. Without one, people
quietly stay at the floor for months and call it sustainable.

## Design around the actual failure points

Three things break routines more often than lack of discipline:

**All-or-nothing equipment.** If your routine requires a gym, a commute to it,
and a free hour, the floor tier cannot exist. Keep a version that needs nothing.
Devices that store easily are more likely to be used than devices that need
setting up or that you hide in an inconvenient place out of caution.

**Invisible progress.** People who track something stick to routines longer, and
the tracking does not need to be sophisticated — a mark on a paper calendar
works. Make the streak visible where you will see it.

**Flexible timing that becomes no timing.** "Sometime today" means "never" on a
hard day. Attach the floor to an existing anchor: after the morning coffee,
before the evening meal, immediately after the school run. Existing habits carry
new ones.

## What to do after you miss a week

You will miss a week. The rule that matters: **the first day back is Tier 1
only.** The instinct is to return at Tier 3 to make up for lost time, which
almost always produces a second collapse within three days.

Treat the return as a return to the floor, hold the floor for a week, then step
up. The cost of a bad week should be a level, not the whole structure.

## The honest summary

A routine that survives real life is less impressive than a routine designed for
a perfect one, and it produces more over a year. The measure that matters is not
how good your best week was. It is how many weeks you ran at Tier 1 or above
instead of running at nothing.

By that measure, the floor is the most valuable part of the whole system — which
is why it is worth designing it first, and keeping it small enough to defend.
